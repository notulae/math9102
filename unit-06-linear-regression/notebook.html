Unit 6 — Linear Regression¶

Units 4 and 5 asked whether groups differ. Regression asks a wider question: how does an outcome change as one or more predictors change, and how well does a straight line describe that? A continuous predictor gives a rate — so much more outcome per unit of predictor. A categorical one gives a difference between groups. One model holds both.

Every part of this notebook uses one dataset: exam results for four thousand pupils in sixty-five London schools. Five questions, in order:

  1. Does reading at 11 predict exam results at 16? (Model 1)
  2. Do girls and boys with the same reading score differ at 16? (Model 2)
  3. Does the slope differ by sex? (Model 3, an interaction)
  4. Do pupils at single-sex schools score differently from the same sex at mixed schools, at the same reading score? (Model 4)
  5. How far can the standard errors trust four thousand pupils who sit in sixty-five schools?

Checking a model's assumptions — the residuals, influence, collinearity — is Unit 7's. This unit fits models and reads them.

How good is the model? The mean as a baseline¶

Without a predictor, your best guess for any pupil is the mean exam score. The total sum of squares measures how far the scores sit from that mean. The line takes some of that distance away (the model sum of squares); what is left is the residual sum of squares.

In [9]:
ss_total, ss_model, ss_resid = model1.centered_tss, model1.ess, model1.ssr
print(f"total {ss_total:.1f} = model {ss_model:.1f} + residual {ss_resid:.1f}")
print(f"R squared = model / total = {ss_model / ss_total:.3f}")

total 4049.4 = model 1417.5 + residual 2631.9
R squared = model / total = 0.350

F is the model's mean square over the residual mean square: how much the line accounts for per degree of freedom it uses, against how much is left per degree of freedom remaining. It tests one null hypothesis — that the line does no better than the mean.

Read R² as a description of this sample. It is the share of the variation in exam scores the line accounts for here. It is not the share of exam success that reading causes, and a sample with a narrower range of readers would give a smaller R² for the same slope.

  • The slope is the difference in predicted exam score between two pupils one unit apart in reading score. Its interval says which slopes the data are consistent with.
  • The intercept is the prediction when every predictor is zero — here, for a pupil of average reading score. Its p-value tests whether that prediction is zero, which is rarely a question anyone asked. Do not report it as a finding.

Two parallel lines¶

Model 2 gives boys and girls the same slope and different intercepts. The gap between the lines is the girl coefficient, and because the lines are parallel it is the same at every reading score.

Comparing Model 2 with Model 1¶

Does adding girl improve the model? Two nested models — one the other plus something — are compared with an F-test for the extra terms. Both must be fitted on the same rows. Models fitted on different rows differ in their data as well as their predictors, and neither F nor the change in R² can separate the two. These data have no missing values, so the rows are the same; the check below compares the rows themselves rather than assuming it.

Nor can you claim that sex "moderates" the reading slope from a model that never let the slope vary — only a model with the interaction in it can address that question, and this one finds no evidence for it.

With an interaction in the model, the girl coefficient changes meaning: it is the girl–boy gap where standlrt is zero, not at every reading score. The report says so.

Part 4 — Do pupils at single-sex schools score differently?¶

schgend records the type of school: mixed, boys' or girls'. Three categories need two dummies. Model 4 adds school type to Model 2, and it is tempting to read each coefficient "holding the others fixed".

Before any "holding fixed", check the combinations exist.

In [26]:
# Before any model: who attends which type of school?
pd.crosstab(regression["schgend"], regression["girl"])
Out[26]:
girl boy girl

boysch 513 0

No girl attends a boys' school, and no boy a girls' school. So "a boys' school, holding sex fixed" cannot be about a girl: there are none to hold. Within a single-sex school sex never varies, so girls and boys can be compared only in the mixed schools. The model will print a coefficient either way; the cross-tab is what tells you which comparisons the data contain.

Part 5 — How far can the standard errors trust these pupils?¶

Every interval so far treats the pupils as independent of one another. They are not: they sit in sixty-five schools, and pupils in one school share teachers, intake and neighbourhood.

The intraclass correlation¶

The ICC is the share of the variation in exam scores that lies between schools. The design effect says roughly how many times larger the sampling variance of the average exam score is than it would be for the same number of unrelated pupils. It is worked out as if every school were the average size, and these schools are far from that, so read it as a rough guide.

Cluster-robust standard errors¶

Cluster-robust standard errors let pupils in one school be alike, and assume only that schools are independent of one another. The coefficients do not move; only their standard errors do, and with them the intervals and tests, which now use t on the number of schools rather than the number of pupils. How much a coefficient loses depends on how much its predictor clusters.

In [33]:
def by_school(formula):
    return smf.ols(formula, data=regression).fit(
        cov_type="cluster", cov_kwds={"groups": regression["school"]}, use_t=True)

model2_cl = by_school("normexam ~ standlrt + girl")
model4_cl = by_school("normexam ~ standlrt + girl + C(schgend, Treatment('mixedsch'))")

The model describes an association in these data. It does not establish that any of these predictors causes exam score at 16.

Read the two school-type intervals in this paragraph against Part 4's. There, both differences were clearly non-zero. With the clustering acknowledged, both intervals reach to about zero: the boys'-school interval only just excludes it, and the girls'-school interval only just includes it. The two differences are about the same size, so do not report one as real and the other as absent: read the intervals, not which side of .05 each p-value falls. Note too that F's second degree of freedom has fallen from thousands to one less than the number of schools: that is how much independent information the test has.

A repair, not the treatment. The model built for pupils in schools is a multilevel model, which is beyond this module. What you can always do is notice the clustering, and say what it does to your claims.

What to take from this unit¶

  • A regression coefficient is a rate for a continuous predictor and a difference for a categorical one, read holding the model's other predictors constant — and only those.
  • F tests the whole model against the mean; R² says how much of the variation the model accounts for in this sample. It never falls as predictors are added; adjusted R² charges for them.
  • Every coefficient comes with an interval. The intercept is a prediction at zero, rarely a finding.
  • A categorical predictor becomes k − 1 dummies against a reference you name. Check by cross-tabulation that the combinations a model adjusts over exist, and say which comparison each coefficient makes.
  • Build an interaction in the formula. A non-significant one shows the data cannot tell the slopes apart, not that they are equal.
  • Compare nested models on the same rows.
  • Cluster-robust standard errors leave the coefficients alone and widen their standard errors, most for a predictor that varies only between clusters and least for a comparison made within them.
  • No regression on observational data establishes cause, and R² is not a share of the reasons.
